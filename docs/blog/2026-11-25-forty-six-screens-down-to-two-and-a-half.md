# Forty-Six Screens Down to Two and a Half

Opening the open-threads view on a real 2,469-page notebook felt like scrolling through a receipt from a grocery store you did not remember visiting. The page was 42,921 pixels tall—nearly forty-seven desktop screens—and the very first thing staring back from the top of the queue was the owner's own name, repeated as if he were a stranger who owed himself replies.

At first it looked like a styling bug. Maybe the cards were just too tall, or the padding too generous. We mocked up a tighter master-detail split, took a screenshot, and hated it. Putting a sidebar next to the same noisy rows only hid the clutter behind a divider; the real friction was still staring out from every line. Every single row repeated six inline controls—Mark done, Not now, Ignore, Hand to Codex, Hand to Claude, More—before you had even decided whether you cared about the row. Worse, a single deposit email had been written onto three separate pages—the person, the company, and the owner—so it showed up three times in a row. And because `.state/map.json` had not been generated on that synced machine, `owner_record()` returned an empty string, leaving the reader unable to recognize who owned the notebook.

The turn came when we stopped asking how to pack six actions more neatly onto a list row and asked what a human actually does in the first five seconds of opening a to-do list. Almost nobody wants to dispatch a Codex agent from a list row without reading what the other person actually said. On the first screen, you only want to scan by tag, tap a circle to strike through what is already done without the list jumping under your cursor, or step into one item when it needs real thought. Bulk actions are the same: showing checkboxes and a dark toolbar all day punishes the 95% of visits where you just want to tick off two items.

Once we separated the two jobs, everything else snapped into place. Screen one became a quiet, tag-grouped list where each obligation leads with the task itself, merges its duplicate copies across pages into one atomic item, and leaves only a circle to strike through in place—keeping batch controls completely invisible until you ask for multi-select or click a tag group. Clicking any row steps into screen two in the exact same grouped order: the full original quote sits in plain sight without trailing colons from clipped prose, the reply draft is editable beside it, and Codex or Claude wait cleanly in the side column instead of shouting from every line of the list. Fixing `owner_record()` to fall back to the owner's own page marker and addresses made the owner disappear from the counterparty column altogether, and replacing Today's stacked pseudo-card shadows with a clean horizontal strip stopped the card deck from painting over its own summary link.

The page dropped from 42,921 pixels to 2,334—not because we hid information, but because we finally stopped asking the first screen to do the second screen's job.
