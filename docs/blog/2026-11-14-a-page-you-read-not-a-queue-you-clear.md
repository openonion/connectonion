# A page you read, not a queue you clear

*2026-11-14*

The owner opened an organisation in the REM reader and asked a question we
could not answer well: did he open this page to see what he had to do today?

The page said so. It led with "1 thing in Today involves this organisation",
the list above it was grouped "Waiting on you" first, a row carried an overdue
chip, and a table told him who replied fastest. Every one of those was true,
and every one treated the organisation as work. His point was that it is not.
An organisation page is what the notebook has worked out about a place he
deals with: what it is, who he knows there, how it is put together, how the
relationship got here. He reads it the way he reads a page of a book. The
to-do list already has a home, and it is called Today.

We had removed the Today counts and thought we were done. He looked again and
said the design intent itself was wrong: the page still hinted with dots,
badges and a bar-code timeline where it should say things in words. So the
page became an article. One column. An opening paragraph that says what the
organisation is, what it is to him, how many people he knows there and since
when. Then the structure as a tree, organisation to units to people with their
roles. Then a few dated milestones, the projects it connects to, labelled
facts with a website he can click, and sources at the foot. A test now lists
the classes whose only job is to signal state and fails if one appears.

The list went the other way. He wanted it to work like the People table, and
to use the width: kind, what they do, website, location, people known,
activity, last contact, each sortable. We looked at how the CRMs handle a
university with schools and teams under it. Most do not: one shows parent and
child as links in a sidebar, one level at a time; another keeps the parent as
text in a cell. The one real tree grid sits behind an action menu. Ours puts
the units under their parent in the main table.

Two things the tests did not tell us. The demo notebook had four flat
organisations, so the tree we had built never showed; the owner said the
structure was missing, and on that data he was right. And on his real
notebook 6 of 28 organisations had a website and 4 a location, so a table of
facts is mostly dashes until the night fills them. Both are now issues of
their own. A page can meet its acceptance list and still have nothing on it.

The same release folds the reader's two record places into one Knowledge
place, removes the sentences that explained each page's order, and opens
Projects on a board by state.
