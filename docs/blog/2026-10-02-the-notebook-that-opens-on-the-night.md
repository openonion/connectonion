# The notebook that opens on the night

The owner opened his co rem notebook to find a phone number. He knew the
page: a co-founder, investigated and full of cited facts. He scrolled
past a lead paragraph, five sections of prose and a history, and did not find
it, because the page had no phone number and nothing said so. A missing
answer and a well-hidden one looked the same.

The reader was clean. A sidebar, a serif heading, a green accent, lists of
names. It was also a table of contents for a wiki, and nobody comes back to
their own memory to read a table of contents. They come back to pick up where
they left off with someone, to see who is waiting on whom, or to find one fact
fast.

So we started from the name. co rem is named for REM sleep: overnight it reads
the day's mail and coding sessions and rewrites the pages they touch. The home
now opens on that night, in the one dark band on an otherwise quiet page: what
the last pass read, which pages it rewrote, and the week's runs drawn as a
hypnogram, a dip for each run, deeper the more it read, an amber dot where one
stopped early. Under it, what is owed, split the way you act on it: waiting on
you, oldest first with its age, and waiting on others.

Then the page the phone number should have been on. It now opens on its
lead and one line: what is open, in which direction, for how many days. Its
facts sit beside the prose as a list with every label present, so an empty
phone field reads "— not found" instead of being absent. People open as a
sheet you can sort by last contact or filter to "yours to answer", because a
list of 330 names is not something you read.

The first render on a copy of the real notebook caught the reader lying. It
said 57 threads were open and none were waiting on the owner. The pages wrote
the owner's first name ("X promised"), not "the user owes", and the direction rule only knew the
second phrasing. A test notebook written by us agreed with our rule; a
notebook written by the investigation did not. The rule now reads the owner's
own name, and the earliest phrase that says who must act decides.

The lesson: design the reader around the question that brings someone back,
and check it against real pages, because an invented notebook only ever says
what you expected it to.
