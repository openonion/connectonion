# Why four good prototypes made one muddy page

We had four striking visual prototypes for the `co rem` reader open side by side in browser tabs, and every one of them worked in its own window. One leaned into deep nocturnal instrument cards, another had crisp architectural rules, a third used bold ultramarine, and the fourth felt like a bound paper ledger. Instead of choosing one and letting the others go, we did the greedy thing and tried merging the strongest pieces of all four into a single REM interface. What came back on screen was visual soup. Every card, chip, and rail talked at the same volume. Worse, where we layered dark purple over black for the overnight sleep panel, the shadow turned muddy, bruised, and strangely oppressive to look at first thing in the morning. Right beside it, our overdue obligation markers in standard alarm red felt like a server outage rather than a gentle nudge about a reply waiting on your desk.

For an afternoon we tried rescuing the page the way engineers usually debug bad colors, nudging hex values a few steps lighter or duller by hand and hitting reload. Everything grew greyer without growing calmer. The turn came when we stopped eyeballing swatches in isolation, wrote a small script to measure pixel chroma across a full rendered viewport, and looked closely at what retinal lateral inhibition was doing to our eyes. Cranking up a badge's saturation cannot rescue it on a noisy page; photoreceptors in the retina only sharpen a tiny patch of color when the surrounding field stays cool and neutral enough to stop competing with it. Our warm off-white surfaces and brownish greys were quietly leaking red-channel warmth across the entire background, forcing overdue markers to scream in fire-engine red just to stand apart. At the same time, tinting dark panels with purple pushed red and blue above green, starving the green channel that human vision relies on for crisp luminance edges and turning deep shadow into plum mud.

Once we treated the viewport as an optical budget instead of a collage of favorite parts, the mud cleared immediately. Eighty percent of the page became Cold Porcelain (`B >= G >= R`), stripping every trace of warm cream from the paper, inset wells, and hairlines so that two percent or less of pure signal color could snap into focus without shouting. All of our scattered dark chrome consolidated into one deliberate sleep and execution slab in pure unmuddied Obsidian (`#0d1315`), where the overnight REM hypnogram draws its active trace in Lucid Viridian (`#007a5a` on porcelain, `#00dc96` on dark surfaces). Replacing alarm red with a quiet one-percent note of Rose-Pink (`#d41e63` by day and `#ff5492` for the dawn awakening dot on Obsidian) turned overdue threads back into a calm morning prompt you actually want to open over coffee.

We still hated throwing those other prototypes away, though. Each one had been coherent on its own; they only fell apart when forced to share one canvas. Because components in the reader already referenced semantic roles rather than raw colors, we wired `#3470` around a pluggable `[data-skin]` token architecture at the top of the sheet. Porcelain and Viridian now own the default room, while our ultramarine, bookcraft, and classic monochrome studies live alongside it as clean skin swaps, and future palettes can drop into `:root[data-skin]` without rewriting a single component rule. Blending four strong palettes together will always make mud, because a two-percent signal only speaks clearly when one disciplined room holds the other eighty percent in quiet reserve.
